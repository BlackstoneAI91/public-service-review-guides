# Writing a Dry Cleaning Enquiry When a Form Has One Description Field

A practical guide for Sarawak households and venue managers on using a single description field to ask a clear dry cleaning question about two items, with a before-and-after example and a three-line planning scratchpad.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why one field is enough for a useful dry cleaning question

Many online enquiry forms give you a single box labelled description or message. That can feel limiting when you have two items and more than one question. The good news is that a single field can carry a clear, useful enquiry if you organise it before you type. The goal is not to write everything you know about the items. It is to help the reader understand what you have, what you are asking, and what you still need to know.

A useful description field entry does three jobs. First, it identifies each item in neutral terms, without diagnosing stains or promising outcomes. Second, it states one question that applies to both items, such as whether the service can help with the type of fabric or item. Third, it flags one item-specific question that you have not yet answered, so the reader knows what to respond to. This structure keeps the message short while giving the reader enough context to reply meaningfully.

For a Sarawak household or venue manager, this approach also respects the limits of a web form. You are not writing a full cleaning brief. You are starting a conversation. The same discipline applies whether you are asking about dry cleaning services for a pair of curtains or a formal outfit. Keep the focus on the items and the question, not on assumptions about what the service will do.

## A three-line scratchpad to plan your description

Before you open the form, spend two minutes with a simple three-line scratchpad. This is a planning tool, not a formal document. It helps you separate what you know from what you are asking.

Line 1: Item A in neutral words. Write only what you can see or know without judging condition. For example, “one pair of lined curtains, approximately 2 kg” or “one long-sleeved formal dress with beadwork.” Avoid words like “ruined” or “delicate” unless the care label or a professional has used them.

Line 2: Item B in neutral words. Use the same approach. “One pair of white school shoes” or “one plush toy, about 30 cm.” If you are unsure of the material, say so: “fabric unknown.”

Line 3: Your shared question and your unanswered item-specific question. The shared question might be “Can you confirm whether this type of item is accepted for dry cleaning services?” The item-specific question might be “For the plush toy, do you need to know whether it has a battery compartment?” Write both in one line so you can see whether they are clear and separate.

This scratchpad is an author heuristic, not a researched method. It works because it forces you to name the items and the questions before you write the final message. You can keep it beside you as you fill in the form.

## Before and after: a fictional Sarawak shopper’s enquiry

Here is a before-and-after example using a fictional Sarawak shopper. The shopper has two items and one form field. The names and items are invented for illustration only.

Before: “Hi, I have some curtains and a dress that need cleaning. The curtains are quite dirty and the dress has a stain. Can you tell me how much and when it will be ready? Also, do you clean soft toys?”

This message mixes items, asks several questions, and includes assumptions about condition. The reader cannot easily tell which question belongs to which item.

After: “Item A: one pair of lined curtains, approximately 2 kg, fabric label missing. Item B: one long-sleeved formal dress with beadwork, care label attached. Shared question: are both items accepted for dry cleaning services? Unanswered item-specific question: for the curtains, do you need the measurements before you can confirm? I have not yet asked about soft toys.”

The after version is longer but clearer. It describes each item neutrally, asks one shared question, and flags one unanswered question. It also notes what the shopper has not asked, which prevents the reader from guessing. The shopper can then submit the form and wait for a reply. No price, timing, or outcome is promised in the message, and none should be assumed from this example.

## What to leave out of the description field

A single description field is not the place for everything you know about your items. Leaving out certain details makes your enquiry easier to answer and reduces the risk of misunderstanding.

Do not include cleaning instructions. You are asking a question, not telling the service how to do its job. If you have a care label, you can mention that it exists, but you do not need to copy it into the field unless the reader asks.

Do not diagnose the condition of the item. Words like “mouldy,” “burnt,” or “hopeless” are judgements. Instead, describe what you see in plain terms: “dark marks along the hem” or “a small tear near the seam.” This keeps the enquiry factual.

Do not ask for a price or a completion date in the same field unless the form specifically invites it. A single field is better used to establish what you have and what you need to know. Pricing and scheduling can follow once the reader has enough information to respond.

Do not include personal details that are not needed for the enquiry. A name and a way to reply are usually enough. The website terms and services apply when you contact the company or submit an enquiry, so use the form honestly and lawfully. If you are unsure what to include, keep to the three-line scratchpad and leave the rest for the reply.

If you mention a plush toy, the reader can note that soft toy cleaning is a separate service. This does not mean the company will accept every item, but it does mean your question reaches someone who can answer it.

A clear description also helps the reader decide what to ask you next. If you have flagged an unanswered item-specific question, the reply can address it directly. If you have left out cleaning instructions and condition diagnoses, the reader is less likely to misunderstand your meaning. ## A short checklist before you press submit

Before you submit your single-field enquiry, run through this short checklist. It is an author heuristic, not a scored test, and you do not need to answer every point perfectly.

1. Have you named each item in neutral words, without diagnosing condition?
2. Have you stated one question that applies to both items?
3. Have you flagged one item-specific question that you have not yet answered?
4. Have you avoided cleaning instructions, price requests, and completion dates unless the form invites them?
5. Have you kept personal details to what is needed for a reply?
6. Have you read your message once to check that a stranger could understand which item is which?

If you can answer yes to most of these, your description is likely to be useful. If not, return to the three-line scratchpad and revise. The aim is not a perfect message but a clear one. A clear message respects the reader’s time and makes it easier for them to respond with the information you need. For dry cleaning services in Kuching and Miri, a short, well-organised enquiry is often the most practical way to start.
