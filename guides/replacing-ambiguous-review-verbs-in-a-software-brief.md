# Rewriting Vague Approval Verbs in a Software Application Development Brief

A practical guide for Malaysian service businesses on separating review, recommend and approve in a software brief, with three rewritten rows and a sign-off worksheet that assigns each decision to a person by role.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why "Approved" Is the Most Misread Word in a Brief

A small change request lands in a brief with a single line: "Approved by management." The writer moves on. Two weeks later, the person who wrote that line says they only meant the wording looked fine, while the person who expected to sign off says nobody asked them. The change stalls, and the brief gets blamed for something the language caused.

The problem is not that the team is careless. It is that English lets one verb carry several jobs. Review, recommend and approve describe three different acts, performed by different people, at different moments. When a brief collapses them into one word, the reader supplies their own meaning, and the meaning they supply is usually the one that suits their role.

A useful habit is to treat every approval verb as a question: who does this, to what, and what happens next? If the brief cannot answer all three from the sentence alone, the sentence is not finished. This applies whether the work is a form, a booking flow or a report, and it applies to internal change requests as much as to work handed to a software application development company.

This guide uses a fictional Malaysian service business, Reka Jaya Services, to show the difference in practice. Nothing here describes a real client, a real project or a real outcome. The examples are constructed to make the grammar visible, and the worksheet at the end is an author heuristic rather than a tested instrument.

## Three Verbs, Three Jobs: Review, Recommend, Approve

Take the three words apart before rewriting anything.

To review is to examine and comment. A reviewer reads the change request, checks it against what they know, and returns observations. Review produces notes, not permission. A reviewer can say "the wording on the confirmation screen is ambiguous" without deciding whether the change proceeds.

To recommend is to advise a course of action. A recommender weighs the notes and says what they think should happen. Recommendation carries judgement but not authority. It is the sentence "I suggest we keep the current field labels and revisit the layout later."

To approve is to authorise. An approver accepts the change and accepts the consequence of it going ahead. Approval is a decision with a name attached, and it should be recorded with a date and the version of the request it applies to.

A fourth verb, to note, is worth keeping for information only. "Noted" means the reader has seen the item and has no decision to make. It is a useful way to close a loop without implying consent.

When a brief uses one word for all four, the reader cannot tell whether they are being asked for an opinion or handed responsibility. Separating the verbs is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between a comment and a commitment, and it costs one extra column in a table.

## Three Rewritten Rows From a Fictional Change Request

Reka Jaya Services wants a small change to its service enquiry form. Here is how three rows might appear before and after rewriting. The business is invented; the pattern is the point.

Row one, before: "New field approved." After: "Field label 'Preferred branch' reviewed by the service desk lead, who noted that two branches share a name. Recommendation from the operations executive: use the branch code instead. Approval to proceed rests with the business owner, recorded against version 3 of this request." The rewrite names three people by role, three acts, and one decision.

Row two, before: "Marketing to confirm." After: "Marketing reviewer to comment on the confirmation message wording by the stated review point. Marketing does not approve the field itself; that decision sits with the business owner. If marketing raises no objection, the reviewer records 'noted' rather than 'approved'." This row removes an accidental veto and an accidental permission at the same time.

Row three, before: "Technical side okay." After: "Technical reviewer confirms the change can be built as described and flags any dependency. Technical recommendation is advisory. Approval remains with the business owner, who accepts that the change affects the enquiry flow." The technical voice is preserved without being promoted into authority.

Notice that none of the rewrites add technology instructions, prices or dates. They add roles, acts and a record. That is the whole edit.

## A Sign-Off Worksheet That Assigns Decisions by Role

A worksheet works better than a paragraph because it forces each row to name a person by role and a single act. The following structure is a suggested template, not a standard.

Column one: item. Describe the change in one sentence, such as "add preferred branch field to enquiry form." Column two: reviewer. Name the role that examines the item and the kind of notes expected. Column three: recommender. Name the role that advises, and state what they are advising on. Column four: approver. Name exactly one role with authority to authorise, and state what they are accepting. Column five: record. Note the version of the request and the date the decision was recorded. Column six: status. Use a small closed set: open, noted, recommended, approved, returned.

The discipline is in column four. If two roles appear there, the item has two approvers and the brief should say how a disagreement is resolved. If none appear, the item cannot be approved and will drift.

A second discipline sits in column six. "Noted" and "approved" must never be treated as synonyms, because one closes a conversation and the other opens work. Teams that keep the set small find it easier to scan a long brief at a glance.

For businesses commissioning outside help, the worksheet travels with the brief. A software application development company reading it can see who decides what without guessing, which reduces the round of clarifying messages that usually follows a vague request.

## Where Briefs Usually Leak Authority

Certain phrases recur in vague briefs, and each one hides a decision. "Subject to confirmation" does not say who confirms. "Final sign-off" does not say what is being signed. "No objection" quietly converts silence into consent. "As discussed" points at a conversation nobody wrote down.

A practical response is to keep a short list of banned or flagged phrases and require each one to be replaced with a role and an act. The list does not need to be long. Five or six phrases cover most of the damage.

A second leak is the meeting note. A note that records "team agreed" without naming who agreed leaves the reader to assume everyone did. Rewriting it as "operations executive recommended; business owner approved; service desk lead noted" takes one line and removes the ambiguity.

A third leak is version drift. A change approved against version two of a request may not be approved against version five. Recording the version beside the decision is a small habit that prevents a large argument later.

None of this requires new software. It requires the brief to treat approval as an event with a person, a scope and a record, rather than as a mood. Teams that adopt the habit often find that fewer items sit in limbo, because limbo is where unnamed decisions go.

## Keeping the Habit Without Turning It Into Ceremony

The risk of any sign-off discipline is that it grows into a ritual that slows small changes. A few limits keep it proportionate.

First, scale the worksheet to the change. A wording fix may need one reviewer and one approver. A change that alters how enquiries are routed may need all three roles. The template stays the same; the number of filled rows changes.

Second, keep the status set closed and short. Five states are enough for most small requests, and a longer list invites debate about which state applies.

Third, review the brief's language at the same time as its content. A brief can be technically complete and still unreadable if every second line says "approved" without saying by whom.

Fourth, treat the worksheet as a living record rather than a form to be filed. Its value is in the moment someone asks who decided, and the answer is one row away.
