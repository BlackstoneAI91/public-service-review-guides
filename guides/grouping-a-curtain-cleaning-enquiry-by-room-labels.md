# Grouping a Curtain Cleaning Enquiry When Two Rooms Share a Name

## Why a Shared Room Name Breaks a Written Enquiry

A written enquiry only works if the reader can point to the same room the writer meant. When two spaces in one building carry the same name, that certainty disappears. The words are identical, so the reader has no way to tell which room the writer had in mind, and any reply risks describing the wrong curtains.

This guide uses a fictional Sarawak community venue, which we will call the venue throughout, with two rooms both labelled Meeting Room A. One sits on the ground floor near the entrance; the other is upstairs beside the storeroom. Nothing here describes a real building, and the labels are invented to make the problem visible.

A room reference sheet is a small, plain document that sits beside the enquiry. It gives each space a unique reference, a short description and a note of what the writer means by it. The sheet does not replace the enquiry; it anchors it. When a reader opens the enquiry, the sheet tells them which Meeting Room A is which.

The value is not administrative tidiness for its own sake. It is that a curtain cleaning services request can be answered against a named space rather than a guess. ## Building the Room Reference Sheet

Keep the sheet short enough to read in one pass. Four columns are usually enough: a unique reference, the everyday name, a short locator in plain words, and the writer's note about what is in the room. The reference does the sorting; the other columns help a human confirm it.

For the fictional venue, the references might read MR-A-GF and MR-A-1F. The everyday name stays Meeting Room A for both, because that is what people say out loud. The locator is deliberately vague and non-operational: ground floor near the entrance, and upper floor beside the storeroom. Do not put door codes, key locations or access steps on the sheet; those belong in a separate operational document, not in a cleaning enquiry.

The note column is where the writer records what they mean. For the ground-floor room it might say two long window panels facing the front. For the upper room it might say one wide window and a short side panel. These are descriptions, not measurements, and they are there to help a reader confirm the match rather than to specify work.

Finally, add a line at the top of the sheet stating its purpose: this sheet identifies rooms for a written enquiry and does not describe cleaning methods, timing or cost. That single sentence keeps the sheet from drifting into scope it was never meant to carry.

## An Ambiguous Note and a Corrected One

Here is how the problem looks in practice. The ambiguous note reads: Please quote for the curtains in Meeting Room A. We have two windows and the fabric is light. A reader cannot tell which room is meant, and the phrase two windows could describe either space depending on how the writer counts.

The corrected note reads: Please quote for the curtains in Meeting Room A, reference MR-A-1F, the upper room beside the storeroom. One wide window and one short side panel, light fabric. The everyday name is kept, the reference is added, and the short description lets the reader confirm the match without needing access details.

The difference is not length. It is that the corrected note gives the reader a way to check. The ambiguous note asks the reader to assume; the corrected note invites a confirmation.

A useful habit is to write the reference first and the everyday name second, so the sorting happens before the description. If a note cannot be corrected before sending, mark it clearly as unresolved rather than letting a guess stand in for a fact. A labelled gap is easier to answer than a confident error.

## A Reply Field for Unresolved Scope

Some enquiries arrive with a genuine gap that the writer cannot close alone. Rather than force a decision, give the reply a small field for unresolved scope. It can be as simple as three lines: what was asked, what remains unclear, and what the writer will confirm next.

For the fictional venue, an unresolved entry might read: Asked about curtains in Meeting Room A. Unclear which room, because two spaces share the name. Will confirm the reference and the window count before the request is treated as complete. That entry is honest and it gives the reader something to respond to.

Keep the field narrow. It is for scope questions, not for scheduling, access or payment discussion. If a question touches those areas, note that it is handled separately and leave the field for the room-matching issue alone.

A reply field also protects the writer from over-claiming. It records that the enquiry is partial by design, not by accident. When the missing detail arrives, the entry is updated and the enquiry becomes whole. This is a writing discipline, not a service promise, and it does not commit anyone to a particular outcome.

## Keeping the Sheet and the Enquiry Consistent

A reference sheet only helps if it matches the enquiry it travels with. Two small checks prevent most drift. First, every reference used in the enquiry should appear on the sheet, spelled the same way. Second, every reference on the sheet should be used somewhere, or marked as not part of this request.

When a venue has many rooms, resist the urge to list them all. A sheet that covers only the rooms in the current enquiry is easier to read and less likely to be misread. Add rooms as they come up, and retire references that are no longer used rather than leaving them to confuse a later reader.

It also helps to date the sheet and note who prepared it, so a reader can tell whether they are looking at the current version. If the everyday names change, keep the old name in a short history line rather than deleting it, because older enquiries may still use it.

## A Short Checklist Before Sending

Before the enquiry leaves, run a short checklist. Each item is a yes-or-no question, and the score is an author heuristic rather than a measured standard.

Does every room in the enquiry have a unique reference? Is the everyday name kept alongside it? Does the locator avoid access details? Does the note describe what the writer means without specifying methods or cost? Is there a reply field for anything still unresolved? Does the sheet carry a date and a preparer?

If any answer is no, fix that item before sending. A single unresolved reference is better labelled than hidden, and a labelled gap is easier to answer than a silent one.
