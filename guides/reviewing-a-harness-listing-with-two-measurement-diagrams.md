# Two Size Diagrams on One Dog Harness Listing: What to Ask Before You Choose

A practical worksheet for Malaysian shoppers comparing a dog harness online listing where two different size diagrams appear, so you can confirm which image belongs to the option you selected before checkout.

## Why Two Diagrams Appear on the Same Listing

A single product page can carry more than one size diagram for reasons that have nothing to do with trickery. A seller may have refreshed the artwork and left the older image in the gallery. A listing may bundle several variants under one page, with each variant drawn to its own diagram. A marketplace template may pull in a generic chart that was never edited for this item. Whatever the cause, the practical problem is the same: you cannot tell which picture the seller intends you to read for the option you have chosen.

This guide is written for Malaysian shoppers comparing third-party listings, often late at night on a phone, where the gallery scrolls faster than the written description. It does not tell you how to measure a dog or how a harness should fit. It focuses on one narrower question: how do you identify which image belongs to your selected option, and how do you get the seller to confirm it in writing before you pay?

## Read the Caption Before the Picture

The fastest way to separate two diagrams is to read what sits directly under or beside each image, not the image itself. Captions, alt text and small grey labels are where sellers usually record which variant an image belongs to, and they are also where mismatches show up first.

Here are two invented listing snippets to show the pattern. These are illustrative examples written for this guide, not real listings.

Snippet A: Gallery image 2 is captioned "Size chart — current version" with no variant name. Gallery image 4 is captioned "Size guide (archive)" and shows a different set of rows. The option selector above the gallery reads "Colour: Sand". Nothing on the page ties either diagram to Sand.

Snippet B: Gallery image 1 is captioned "Chart for model HS-2, all colours". Gallery image 3 is captioned "Chart for model HS-4". The option selector reads "Model: HS-4". Here the caption at least names a model, so you can match it to your selection.

In both snippets, the caption is doing the work the picture cannot. Write down the exact caption text, including any words like current, archive, updated or all colours. Those words are clues about which image the seller considers live. If a caption is missing entirely, treat that image as unidentified rather than assuming it is the right one.

## Match the Visible Option to the Visible Image

Your selected option is whatever the page shows as chosen in the selector, not what you intended to choose. Screenshot the selector and the gallery together so the pairing is captured in one frame. Then ask three plain questions of the page.

First, does either diagram name the option you selected? If the selector says a colour and the diagrams name only models, the link is unproven. Second, do the two diagrams differ in a way that changes which row you would read, or are they simply two drawings of the same information? If they differ, you need the seller to say which one applies. Third, is there a written description elsewhere on the page that names a diagram, such as "see the second image"? Written references outrank your guess about which picture looks newer.

This is also where a dog harness online purchase differs from browsing a shelf. In a physical shop you can point at the item and the chart together. Online, the pairing between option and image is something you have to reconstruct and record. If you are comparing several harness listings at once, keep one note per listing rather than one combined note, so a caption from one page does not drift into another.

## A Worksheet for Recording What You See

Use a simple four-column worksheet, one row per diagram. This is an author heuristic for organising evidence, not a scoring system and not a test of the seller.

Column one, image position: for example, gallery image 2. Column two, caption text: copy it exactly, including brackets and qualifiers. Column three, visible option: the option shown as selected when you took the screenshot. Column four, seller's written confirmation: leave blank until the seller replies, then paste their exact words.

A filled example row might read: image 2 | "Size chart — current version" | Colour: Sand | Seller reply pending. A second row: image 4 | "Size guide (archive)" | Colour: Sand | Seller reply pending.

The blank fourth column is the point of the worksheet. It stops you from treating your own reading of a caption as the seller's statement. Keep the worksheet with your screenshots so that, if a parcel arrives and the question comes up again, you can see what the page said on the day you ordered. ## What to Ask the Seller, and What a Good Reply Contains

Send one short message with your screenshots attached and ask a closed question. For example: "I have selected Colour: Sand. Two size diagrams appear in the gallery, image 2 captioned current version and image 4 captioned archive. Which diagram applies to the Sand option I have selected?"

A useful reply names the image, names the option and states the relationship in one sentence. A weak reply says "please refer to the chart" without saying which chart, or repeats the whole gallery. If the reply is ambiguous, ask again with the image number quoted back. Keep the reply in your worksheet's fourth column.

Two limits are worth stating plainly. First, a seller's confirmation tells you which image they intend for your option; it does not by itself verify the contents of that image. Second, this page does not advise on fit, measurement, use, safety, pricing or care, and a confirmation message is not a substitute for the manufacturer's own documentation. Where a listing and a manufacturer document disagree, note both and ask the seller which one they are working from. ## Before You Check Out, and Where to Read Next

Run a short closing check. Confirm that your worksheet has at least one row per diagram. Confirm that the visible option in your screenshot matches the option in your cart. Confirm that the seller's reply names an image number rather than a vague reference. If any of those is missing, the honest position is that the listing has not yet told you which diagram belongs to your selection.

It also helps to record the date and time of your screenshots, because listings change. A caption that read archive today may read current next week, and your note should reflect what you actually saw rather than what the page says later.
